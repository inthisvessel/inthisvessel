A behavioral design framework, tested in a real clinic.

Four stages, in order. Each card shows the stage and what it looked like when I rebuilt the specialist referral process at Mercy Health Center.

Name the real problem

The cause, not the symptom.

Patients weren't the problem. Five failures were: no shared mental model, one-size-fits-all packets, no tracking layer, unsupported patient effort, and no closure point.

Don't just read about it

Run the morning triage yourself.

A playable version of the referral tracker I built at Mercy. One row is one referral, and no row is allowed to go quiet. Move referrals forward, let a day pass, then triage. Turn on the notes to see why each piece exists.

01
One row, one referral.

Every active referral is visible in one place, so nothing lives only in someone's memory.

02
Every row has a next action.

A status alone doesn't move anything. A named next step does.

03
Quiet rows surface first.

Triage sorts by days without movement, so stalled referrals can't hide.

04
"Patient seen" is the finish line.

The old process had no closure point. Now a referral is done only when care happens.

Principles

What the work keeps teaching me.

Each one came from a real project, not a poster.

Find the cause, not the symptom.

"Patients don't follow through" was the symptom. "No one owns the referral" was the cause. Design for the second one.

Seen in Closing the Care Gap →

Carry the load people can't.

Asking patients to gather documents alone assumed effort they couldn't spare. So I sat with them and filled out applications in person.

Seen in Closing the Care Gap →

Keep the brand. Remove the barrier.

Accessibility work doesn't mean erasing identity. The remote kept its orange; only the contrast changed.

Seen in Rethink the Remote →

Design it to outlast you.

A fix that depends on one person isn't a system. SOPs, a tracker and partner relationships kept referrals closing.
