The problem

A process existed on paper, but not in practice.

Patients weren't missing specialty care because providers didn't care. They were getting lost inside a referral process that no one truly owned.

Research

Understanding a system no one could explain.

There was no documented workflow to study. I rebuilt it by following referrals from start to finish, interviewing providers, and learning how each specialist office actually accepted patients.

UNDERSTANDING SPECIALIST WORKFLOWS

In my first two weeks I called the specialist offices Mercy referred to and asked each the same questions:

  1. What is your financial aid application, and what counts as complete?
  2. What clinical documentation do you require with each referral?
  3. What is your preferred method of contact: fax, phone, email or portal?
  4. Who should I reach for financial aid questions, and what is their direct line?
UNDERSTANDING INTERNAL WORKFLOWS

Referral failures were driven less by patient behavior than by inconsistent processes.

Every specialist office operated differently, ownership shifted between people, and no one had visibility once a referral left the clinic.

What I learned

Five failures. One design brief.

These weren't isolated mistakes. They were predictable symptoms of a process with no shared ownership, no visibility and no standard workflow. Tap a card to see what was going on.

These five failures became the design brief for what came next.

Solution

No workflow existed, so I built one.

Five components and one coordinator. Together they turned lost referrals into closed loops.

Step-by-step process document

A standard operating procedure

The SOP defined who does what, in what order, and where the handoffs live. It also made the Referrals Coordinator role the single point of accountability. Referrals were no longer "everyone's job and therefore no one's job."

Single source of truth

A master tracking spreadsheet

A central Excel tracker gave every active referral a visible status. It became the engine of the daily workflow: no referral could stall silently, because every row carried a date and a next action.

Patient prep guide

The cognitive load, broken into steps
  1. Book a short in-person session. Bring any ID you have.
  2. We gather your information and documents.
  3. We fill out the aid application line by line.
  4. I send the packet and call you when the appointment is set.
Reusable patient-facing materials

In-person document assembly

Instead of handing patients a list of documents to gather alone, I sat with each one in dedicated in-office appointments and we filled out applications together. For patients who couldn't easily come back, I made the calls and gathered documents for them. This part of the system took on the cognitive and logistical load the old process had quietly pushed onto patients.

Trust with patients and partners

Daily follow-up and partner relationships

Each morning I triaged every open row by next-action date. Standing meetings with specialist partners turned one-off contacts into relationships that moved referrals faster.

THE FIRST SIGNAL IT WAS WORKING

By the end of the first month, the new process produced what the old one couldn't: closed referrals. Patients who had waited over a year started getting scheduled, including a patient with cirrhosis. Rows moved through statuses instead of stalling at "Open," and the backlog shrank week over week.

What changed structurally

The shift wasn't only in throughput. Mercy's relationships with its specialist partners changed too. Where the clinic used to send packets into a void, it now had a named point person who knew each office's financial aid contact by name and could move cases in days rather than weeks.

The outcome I'm most proud of

The system outlived my role. When I left Mercy, the SOP, the tracker, the checklists and the daily cadence stayed in use. A process that depends on one person is fragile. A process that keeps running after that person leaves is a system. Mercy now has one.

What it meant for patients

Most case studies I read in school were about conversion rates. This one was about whether a patient with cirrhosis would see a hepatologist before their disease progressed.

The patients who had waited longest were the first to benefit, including that patient, who was scheduled and seen in the first cycle of the new process. Many told me they had stopped expecting the system to work at all. The redesign was built, in part, to earn that expectation back. It taught me to take design seriously, because the people on the other side of it are taking their lives seriously.

Reflection

Walking into ambiguity

No training, no documented process, a year-plus backlog. Not knowing what to do next isn't the same as having nothing to do. The work was to ask, observe and map until the system's shape appeared.

Designing for people, not users

I sat with sick, exhausted people and asked them to gather bank statements. I called the Department of Labor while they waited beside me. The person on the other side of every screen has a body and a limit.

Building systems that outlive you

I could have been the system, holding every relationship in my head and calling it competence. Writing it down so someone else could inherit it was the harder, better choice.

WHAT SURPRISED ME

I expected the fix to be about chasing specialist offices harder. It wasn't. No one owned the handoff, so the highest-leverage move was making every open case visible in one place. Visibility changed behavior more than effort did.

Design tradeoffs I weighed
A lightweight tracker now, not a perfect tool later

An Excel tracker shipped in days and made every open case visible. Waiting on a built-in EHR feature would have meant patients kept waiting, so I chose speed and visibility over elegance.

One named owner first, then documented

Centralizing accountability fixed the void right away, but a one-person process is brittle. Writing the SOP and checklists traded short-term effort for a system that could outlive me.

Triaged by clinical urgency, not arrival order

A year-old backlog can't be worked first in, first out. Sickest-first was less "fair" by queue logic, but it was the only defensible choice with a cirrhosis patient in the stack.

Documented while clearing, not after

Slower throughput in the first weeks bought a process that still ran months later. I treated durability as the real deliverable.
